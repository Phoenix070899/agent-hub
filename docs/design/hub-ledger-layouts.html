Ledger, four ways to lay it out

The look stays the same in all four: cool paper, ink blue, and a highlighter on anything waiting for you. What changes is where the ledger sits next to the terminal, and what the ledger is ordered by. Same demo moment as before, plus a second session so the multi-session layouts have something to show.

What stays fixed

Instrument Sans for the interface, Spline Sans Mono for terminals. Marker yellow is reserved for Needs you and nothing else, so it always means you.

What each layout trades

Terminal size against how much of the ledger you can see at once, and one session in depth against every session at a glance.

No terminal tabs

The ledger is how you move between the Orchestrator and Workers, and shells belong to your multiplexer. So the terminal header only names what it’s showing.

My pick: 3, Day book. It keeps the shape you liked in C (ledger left, terminal right) and fixes its weak spot. Sorting by state hides when things happened, and with several Workers that’s often the question: “what changed while I was away?” If you regularly keep two or three sessions live at once, look hard at 4.

Top sheet

Ledger across the top, terminal full width below

The three sections sit side by side like the columns of a ledger page. The terminal gets the window’s whole width, and the sheet folds to a single line of counts when you want the height back.

Best when

You work on a wide external display, or you want the terminal wide enough for long diffs and test output.

Trade-off

Takes height, which terminals need most. With more than three Workers per section the band has to scroll or grow.

In SwiftUI

  • VStack of a 3-column Grid and PaneTree
  • Fold with a DisclosureGroup-style toggle into a one-line summary
2

Ledger home

The ledger is the page; terminals slide over it

Summary-first taken all the way. The ledger fills the window across every session, and you talk to the Orchestrator from a composer at the foot of the page. A terminal opens as a sheet from the right only when you click into a Worker, then slides away.

Best when

You mostly steer and approve, and rarely read the Orchestrator’s raw output. Feels closest to the Floating Hub button idea at full size.

Trade-off

The composer has to type into the Orchestrator’s terminal for you, which bends ADR 0005, and slash commands or TUI menus still need the real terminal.

In SwiftUI

  • Sectioned List over all snapshots
  • Overlay sheet with .transition(.move(edge: .trailing))
  • New daemon call to send a line to the Orchestrator
3

Day book

Ledger by time, Needs you pinned on top

Keep the original split, but write the ledger like a day book: every handoff, report and request in the order it happened, with what needs you pinned above it. Coming back after lunch, you read down from the top until you reach where you left off.

Best when

You step away and come back. Clicking an entry opens that Worker’s terminal, or the Orchestrator at that moment.

Trade-off

“What’s running right now” is no longer one glance; you read it from the Summary or the latest entries. Needs the daemon to keep an event log with timestamps.

In SwiftUI

  • List with a pinned header section
  • Entries from the daemon’s events stream, stored per session
  • Rail and marks drawn with a small Canvas
4

Session columns

One ledger per session, terminal in a drawer

Built for running several Hub Sessions at once. Each session gets its own ledger column, side by side, so you see every repo’s state without switching. The terminal lives in a drawer at the bottom that shows whatever you last clicked: a Worker, or a session’s Orchestrator.

Best when

Two or three repos are live at once and you’re mostly answering and approving across them. Answers the backlog’s “multiple repos” item without Cmd-1 to Cmd-9.

Trade-off

The terminal is short, so long reads mean dragging the drawer up. Beyond three or four sessions the columns get narrow.

In SwiftUI

  • HStack of per-session Lists, reusing today’s snapshots
  • Drawer is a VSplitView or custom drag handle
  • Drawer remembers its height per window
